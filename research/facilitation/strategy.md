# Facilitation strategy (WP3-D2)

How to run SCAT sessions in the manner of improvised jazz. Sources are in `sources.md`; citations use the same keys:
[ant-rdf] at commit `a2ec48e`, [KOI], [Loop]. Where a line is our own design and not a finding, it is marked
*(our design)*.

## The stance in one page

In scat singing, each singer takes up what the last one sang and adds to it. Two moves carry the whole strategy.

- **"Yes, and".** Whatever someone offers is taken up and built on: written down as offered, then extended by a
  question that adds, not one that tests. Nothing offered is turned away, corrected or merged into someone else's line.
  This matches [ant-rdf], where the questions come one at a time, nothing is pre-sorted, and a person revises the
  rendering in their own words (`CLAUDE.md`, "Authoring flow"; `.claude/skills/ant-mgmt.md`).
- **"From this perspective".** Each contribution is placed in the group it comes from, and said that way: "from where
  the operators sit, ...". [ant-rdf] treats a reading as provenance, not fact, and lets readings that contradict stand
  together (`ONTOLOGICAL_COMMITMENTS.md` C6; `AGENTS.md`, pitfalls 1 and 2).

Three limits keep it honest.
- **Nothing is ranked, voted on or settled.** A facilitator never asks the room to pick a winner among stories or
  architectures, and never asks anyone to concede. Methods for ranking, voting or reaching agreement are out of
  scope for SCAT (R-14).
- **Groups, not individuals.** Sessions run under the Chatham House rule: what is said may be used, but nobody is
  named and no group stands for one identifiable person. Perspectives belong to stakeholder groups.
- **Within the cap.** Each vault sets a cap on the number of stakeholder groups. Participants are clustered into
  groups that fit under it. Clustering is by shared standpoint, not by size or by who is loudest.

Why this matters: boiling many accounts down to one can leave a group misled about its own spread of views (the
supplied drafts); [ant-rdf] plans partial connection of readings rather than their union (`FUTURE_WORK.md`, multiplicity section; a2ec48e). [KOI] and
[Loop] offer theory for plurality (Diffraction; Conclusion) but no live-room method, so the moves below are *(our
design)*, shaped by [ant-rdf]'s authoring manner.

## Scenarios and architectures: two ways of looking at one graph

Separate sessions, or clearly separate halves of one session, look from opposite sides (MISSION.md).
- **Scenarios are behavioural**: people in a situation doing things, with outcomes. Time is central. Technology
  stays in the background; what is heard is what it lets people do.
- **Architectures are structural**: how parts fit into tools that address concerns. Stakeholders are set aside; the
  same concern may come from several groups, and nobody can say who will adopt it.

"Actors" (who tell scenarios) and "technologists" (who pitch architectures) name a device for a session, not a split
of people. The same person may speak in both, from different perspectives.

## J4. Planning an event

**Aim.** Leave with a plan in which every voice has a place to be taken up and nobody is set up to win.

**Steps.**
1. **Choose the sessions.** Decide which sessions elicit scenarios and which elicit architectures. Keep them apart.
   Order is a facilitation choice, not a hierarchy: neither kind is the "real" one.
2. **Gather the standpoints.** List who is likely to come, then cluster them into stakeholder groups by what they
   look at the world from, within the cap. If more standpoints appear than the cap allows, widen a group's
   description to hold them; do not drop a standpoint. Where it is unclear which group someone belongs to, ask
   them, and let a person speak from more than one.
3. **Seat each group in each session.** Every group has a way to speak in the scenario sessions. In the
   architecture sessions, anyone who proposes a design is welcome, and groups that do not propose still have a
   place to say what concerns the design must meet.
4. **Frame under Chatham House.** Say it out loud at the start and write it on the page: what is said may be used,
   no one is named, and a group is never a stand-in for one person. Plan how notes are taken so they hold groups,
   not individuals.
5. **Plan for margins.** Before the event, note where groups seem likely to share a concern yet differ on others.
   These are places to listen for, not conflicts to prevent or resolve *(our design)*. Following the supplied
   drafts, hold them as questions to look at, not findings.
6. **Plan the notes.** Raw notes are kept apart from any later reading of them [ant-rdf] (`ONTOLOGICAL_COMMITMENTS.md`
   C8). Decide who captures, and agree that capture is verbatim in spirit: record what was offered before anyone
   interprets it.

**"Yes, and" at this stage.** The plan takes up each group's proposed standpoint and builds on it. When an invitee
asks for a session or topic, add it; do not trade it off against another. The planner's questions add ("who else
sees it this way?"), not test ("is that really a group?").

**"From this perspective" at this stage.** The plan names each stakeholder group as a standpoint, in a sentence a
member would accept as theirs: "from where we sit, ...". Each session's prompt is worded from a stated perspective,
so no one's wording is treated as the neutral default. [ant-rdf]'s catechism asks, later in its sequence and as an optional but encouraged question, from whose practice the
person is describing this (`CLAUDE.md`, "Authoring flow", step 2; a2ec48e). Our plan asks it first, for the room
*(our design)*.

**Not here.** No step asks groups to agree a shared agenda by weighing topics against each other; topics are
added, not eliminated.

## J5. Capturing: scenarios and architectures, treated separately

The room is the live moment. Both kinds of session use the same two moves; what is asked differs.

### J5a. Scenarios (behavioural)

**Aim.** Hear what people do in a situation, in time, and what comes of it, from the group telling it.

**Moves.**
1. **Invite a story.** Ask from a concrete moment: "Tell us about a time when ...". Questions come one at a time,
   as in [ant-rdf]'s catechism (`.claude/skills/ant-mgmt.md`, "Catechism"). Do not ask the teller to sort what comes
   up into types.
2. **Follow in time.** Ask what happened next, who did what, and what followed. What is heard of technology is what
   it let people do or stopped them doing. Do not ask for how it works.
3. **Ask what concern sits underneath.** "What were you trying to get done, or protect?" This draws out the concern
   a group holds, which a later session can match against architectures.
4. **Ask what was contested or came apart.** The last question in [ant-rdf]'s catechism is what is contested or
   unravelling (`CLAUDE.md`, "Authoring flow", step 2). Ask it, and record whatever comes.
5. **Read it back.** Show the teller's group the note and let them revise it in their words [ant-rdf] (`CLAUDE.md`,
   "Authoring flow", step 5). If they correct it, the correction is added, and the first wording stays.

**"Yes, and".** A second story from the same moment is not a rival to the first. The facilitator says: "Yes, that
happened, and here is what it looked like from where you sit?" Then asks the next group the same of its own story.
When one group's account seems to cut across another's, both are written down in full; the facilitator asks each
what it adds, not which is right.

**"From this perspective".** Every story note opens with its group ("from the point of view of ..."), and every
affordance, outcome and concern named is tied to that group. When a story is retold by someone else, it becomes
their story from their perspective; the original stays as told.

**Holding disagreement.** If two groups tell stories that cannot both go well, note both and name the tension aloud
as a margin: "these two want opposite things from the same moment." Do not ask which should win. [ant-rdf] likewise
treats a contested reading as something to show, not resolve (`briefs/koi-comparison.md`, "Where the frames diverge").

### J5b. Architectures (structural)

**Aim.** Hear how a proposed assemblage is put together, and which concerns it addresses, without deciding if it is right.

**Moves.**
1. **Invite the pitch.** Ask the proposer how the parts fit and what the whole lets people do. Parts and wholes, not
   people. Stakeholders are largely set aside.
2. **Ask which concerns it answers.** "What worry or aim is this for?" Write down each concern on its own, so it can
   be matched with groups later.
3. **Ask what it leaves out.** "What does this not address?" [ant-rdf] states each reading's silence in the reading
   itself (`briefs/koi-comparison.md`), and treats a missing ground as something to display, not hide
   (`ontology/shapes/ant-shapes-warnings.ttl`). Here a gap is a fact about the design, recorded without blame.
4. **Ask who else might hold that concern.** The architecture cannot presume who will adopt it, so the question is
   open, and other groups' answers are welcome later.
5. **Keep the design apart from the stories that prompted it.** A proposed design is its own note, not a conclusion
   from a scenario (our inference from the sources' silence on eliciting designs).

**"Yes, and".** A second architecture for the same concern is a companion, not a challenger. "Yes, that answers it,
and here is another way to answer it?" Several designs for one concern, and several concerns for one design, are all
welcome. No design is asked to justify itself against another.

**"From this perspective".** Each pitch is tied to who proposes it and from what practice ("from where the
developers sit ..."), not as the standard way. A concern is written in the words of the group that voiced it, even
when two groups voice almost the same concern in different words. Do not merge them in the room; that is for a
later reading.

**Holding disagreement.** Two architectures that cannot coexist are both captured. The facilitator may name the
tension, and ask what each protects. [ant-rdf] holds opposed programs side by side (`ontology/material-semiotics-core.ttl`)
and does not prefer one.

### J5, for both

- **Interrupting.** If someone is talked over, return to them: "Yes, and we haven't yet heard what that looks like from
  your side."
- **Silence.** A group that stays quiet is not assumed to agree. Say so in the notes as "no account offered", which is
  a gap to record and not a result.
- **Record.** What was said is captured as raw notes, with the group and time, before anyone reads meaning into it
  (ant-rdf C8; the supplied drafts on origin and time).
- **Sharing.** Anything a group has not agreed to share beyond the room stays in the room *(our design)*. [Loop] treats
  consent as a live boundary: in its Telescope section a withdrawn contribution was deleted while a record of the
  withdrawal stayed, without content.

## J6. What coding needs from capture

Coding happens afterwards in an interactive session, turning raw notes into pages and relations. This is why J5 is
shaped as it is. Coding needs the following from capture; each comes from a move above.

1. **Raw notes that are still raw.** Coding goes from what was offered, not from a summary. Interpretation is a
   separate, later act [ant-rdf] (`adr/0000-foundational-decisions.md` R5; `ONTOLOGICAL_COMMITMENTS.md` C8).
2. **Group on every note.** Without it, nothing can be placed under a stakeholder group.
3. **Concerns in the speaker's words**, one at a time, so coding can relate the same concern to different groups.
4. **Affordances in scenarios; technologies in architectures.** If a scenario note drifted into how a tool works, or
   an architecture note into who uses it, coding can separate them, since the notes kept the two looks apart.
5. **Gaps as recorded facts.** "Not addressed" and "no account offered" become visible gaps, not blanks.
6. **Tensions as named.** A tension noticed aloud in the room is carried across and stays as a tension.
7. **A draft label.** What is coded from raw notes enters as a draft, open to the group it came from to correct or
   withdraw (the supplied drafts on owner correction; [ant-rdf] C7: verification is machine, validation is human).

**"Yes, and" in coding.** The coder takes up every offered line and builds on it with a placeholder or a link; nothing is
dropped for being awkward. When a note does not fit the page types, the coder leaves a placeholder for it and goes on, because an
unfinished page is not an error. Where two notes seem to say the same thing, the coder keeps both and marks them as
related, and does not merge them into one.

**"From this perspective" in coding.** Every coded relation keeps its group. The same concern held by two groups
is one concern, held by two groups, each in its own wording. The coder writes the reading as "under this group's
practice, ..." and never "it is ..." (ant-rdf `AGENTS.md`, pitfall 1). A corrected reading is added next to the old
one; neither overwrites.

**Holding disagreement in coding.** A tension between groups or between concerns is coded as a tension and left open.
If the problem messages from the tools suggest a "fix" that would erase a difference, the fix is to record the
difference and mark the gap with a reason, as [ant-rdf] does with its acknowledged-gap pattern (R9b). Nothing in
coding scores, orders or chooses among concerns, scenarios or architectures.

**Read-back.** Where a group wants to see how its account was coded, show it and take its corrections the same way.

## What not to do (all stages)

- Do not ask a room to choose, order, score or agree between stories or architectures. SCAT has no such step.
- Do not merge two accounts into one.
- Do not treat silence as agreement.
- Do not name individuals or let a group stand for one person.
- Do not exceed the stakeholder-group cap by dropping a standpoint; widen the group.
