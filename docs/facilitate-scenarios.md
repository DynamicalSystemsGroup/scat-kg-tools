# Facilitating scenarios

!!! tip "Your next step"
    Open the session by reading the Chatham House lines aloud, then ask the first group for a story that starts at a concrete moment.

*Each group takes a turn: you listen, then build on what it said.*
{ .epigraph }

This guide borrows "yes, and" from jazz improvisation: you accept what each group offers as it is, and add to it.

A scenario session is a set of stories, one turn per group. It hears what people do in a situation, in time, and what comes of it. You run the session. You ask the questions, say whose turn it is, and listen. In each group's turn, you listen to its story, write it down as offered, then ask a question that adds to it. A note-taker
writes. The lines under "Say" are your script. Their order is fixed: say them in the order given. Their wording is yours: say a line as written or in your own words, except "Yes, and", which you say exactly as written. This page runs you through the session stage by stage, then turns the notes into a raw scenario page.

Nothing here is ranked, voted on or adjudicated. You never ask the room to pick a story, and you never ask anyone to
concede one.

## Before you start

- A plan: groups within your cap, a prompt for each group, a note-taker. See [Planning](planning.md).
- A vault that passes its check, with the tools set up. See [Getting started](getting-started.md).

## The stance

Use two habits in every turn of the session: "Yes, and" and "From this perspective".

- **Yes, and.** Write down whatever someone offers, as offered. Then ask a question that adds to it. Never ask a
  question that tests it. The words "Yes, and" are required wherever a line under "Say" contains them: say those two
  words aloud, exactly, at the start of that question.
  If something offered seems wrong to you, do not correct it or rank it. Write it down as offered, ask what it adds,
  and set another perspective beside it: "from this perspective, ...". Saying "Yes, and" means you accept what was offered and add to it. It does not mean you agree with it. If you think a story is wrong, you can still say: "Yes, and what did that look like from where the other group sits?"
- **From this perspective.** Place every contribution in the group it comes from, and say it that way: "from where
  the operators sit, ...". The group names in the lines under "Say" (the operators, the borrowers) are examples. Use the names of your own groups.

Scenarios are behavioural. Technology stays out of view. What you hear of a tool is what it let people do, or stopped
them doing. That is an affordance. When a technology comes up, ask what the tool let people do or stopped them doing, and write that down, not what the tool is.

## The stages

### 1. Opening

Do: say the Chatham House frame aloud. Say that today is for stories, and that accounts can differ.

The lines under "Say" show each move. Say them as written or in your own words, but when a line uses "Yes, and" or "from this perspective", keep those words and say them aloud.

```text
Whatever is said here can be used. Nobody is named; the
notes say which group an account comes from, never who
gave it. Today is for stories about what people do.
Where two stories differ, we write both. Yes, and:
whatever you tell us will be taken up and added to.
```

Note-taker writes: the date, the session, and "Chatham House: groups, not individuals."

### 2. Eliciting

Do: ask one question at a time, from a concrete moment. Do not ask the teller to sort what comes up into types.

Say these in this order. They are questions. Ask one at a time, then wait for the answer, take it up, and only then ask the next. Do not ask two at once.

```text
Tell us about a time when ...
Yes, and what happened next? Who did what?
What were you trying to get done, or protect?
What was contested, or came apart?
```

If a tool is named, bring it back to the doing: "What did that let you do? What did it stop you doing?" Do not ask how
it works.

Note-taker writes: what was done, in order. Each thing a tool let people do or stopped them doing, as an affordance. Each concern,
in the teller's words, on a line of its own. What was contested.

### 3. Taking up

Do: when a second story comes from the same moment, take it up as a companion. It is not a rival to the first.

Say:

```text
Yes, that happened, and here is what it looked like from
where you sit?
```

Then ask the next group the same question of its own story. When one group's account seems to cut across another's,
write both in full. Ask each what it adds, not which is right.

### 4. Placing in perspective

Do: say the group aloud every time you turn to someone, and open each note with it.

Say:

```text
From where the borrowers sit, what did that morning look
like?
```

Note-taker writes: `From the point of view of GROUP NAME (about 10:20)` at the head of each account. Every affordance, outcome and concern
is written under that group. When someone retells a story, it becomes their story from their perspective, and the
original stays as told.

### 5. Capturing and reading back

Do: show the teller's group the note and let them revise it in their own words. If they correct it, add the correction
beneath, and leave the first wording in place.

Say:

```text
Here is what we wrote down from where you sit. What
would you add or change?
```

### 6. Closing

Do: tell the room what happens to the notes, and ask whether anything should stay in the room. After the session, bring
each group its page, as a paper or screen copy, and add its corrections beneath the first wording.

Say:

```text
The notes become a page in a private vault.
After the session we will bring you the page,
and you can add or change anything on it.
Yes, and is there anything you want kept in the room?
```

Note-taker writes: anything a group asks to withdraw is left out of the notes, with a line saying that it was withdrawn and no
content.

## Holding disagreement, silence and interruption


Do: if two groups tell stories that cannot both go well, write both and name the tension aloud as a margin. Do not ask
which should win.

Say:

```text
These two want opposite things from the same moment.
Yes, and let's hear what each one is protecting.
```

Note-taker writes: "Tension: ..." with both accounts kept, each under its own group.

If two groups hold the same concern in different words, write each in its own words. Do not merge them in the room.

If a group stays quiet, do not take that as agreement. Write "no account offered" under that group. That is a gap you
record, not a result.

If someone is talked over, return to them:

```text
Yes, and we haven't yet heard what that looks like from
your side.
```

## Take the notes into a raw scenario page

Notes are taken under Chatham House: groups, no individuals, no attribution. A good set of notes looks like this:

```text
Chatham House: groups, not individuals.

From the point of view of Operators (about 10:20)
- Saturday, desk opens at 9. Three people ask for the same drill.
- Concern: "we cannot lend a tool we have not checked."
- A booking sheet lets us see who has what. It stops us seeing tools that are late.

From the point of view of Borrowers (about 10:35)
- We queued, and nobody said how long.
- Concern: "I need it for the afternoon, not next week."

Tension: Operators want a check before a tool leaves. Borrowers want it in hand now.

Trustees and funders: no account offered.
```

One raw page holds one story, with the accounts of every group that spoke to it, each under its own group, as in the notes above. A different story gets its own raw page.

Keep to the rules the notes carry:

- the group heads every account, and no name appears;
- tools appear only as what they let people do;
- concerns are in the speaker's words, one per line;
- do not link other pages with `[[...]]`. Coding adds the links.

Make the raw page with `scat stub`. It checks the name for you and writes the front matter, so you never hand-write
structure. Give it a name of lowercase letters, digits and hyphens.

=== "Python"

    ```python
    import scat_kg
    scat_kg.stub("lending-desk-saturday", type="scenario-raw")
    ```

=== "CLI"

    ```text
    uv run scat stub --type scenario-raw lending-desk-saturday
    ```

The tool prints:

```text
created scenario-raw/lending-desk-saturday.md
```

The page is made, with status `stub`:

```text
---
type: scenario-raw
status: stub
title: "Lending desk saturday"
---
```

Open the page in Obsidian or any text editor and paste your notes under the front matter, as they were taken. Do not tidy them. Then
mark the page as a draft and check it:

=== "Python"

    ```python
    scat_kg.set_status("lending-desk-saturday", "draft")
    scat_kg.check()
    ```

=== "CLI"

    ```text
    uv run scat set-status lending-desk-saturday draft
    ```

    ```bash
    uv run scat check
    ```

Setting the status prints two lines:

```text
changed scenario-raw/lending-desk-saturday.md
note: `status` set to draft (was stub)
```

The check prints this, from either tab:

```text
warning [SCAT-CONFLICT-BOTH] scenario-coded/notes-at-a-chatham-house-session-coded.md:11 field `concerns`: this record holds both [[anonymity]] and [[attribution]], which conflict (declared on [[anonymity]])
  Fix: none needed if both belong in this record. Holding both sides is allowed and is what the warning marks, a place where two concerns pull apart. If one was coded here by mistake, delete its line `- "[[anonymity]]"` or `- "[[attribution]]"` under `concerns`.
warning [SCAT-DRAFT-FIELD] scenario-raw/lending-desk-saturday.md field `description`: missing (required once the page is done)
  Fix: add the line `description: "<one sentence you write>"` (author) before you set status: done
0 errors, 2 warnings
```

These warnings are a nudge, not a fault. The page is a draft. It is finished when you code it.
See [Coding](coding.md).

`scat stub` is the one way to create a page, because it refuses a bad name and says why.
